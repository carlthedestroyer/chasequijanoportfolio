Modular Flexible PCB for Self-Reconfigurable Soft Robotics

Undergraduate Researcher · Rowan Robotics Lab (PI: Dr. Mitja Trkov) · 2025

Designed the electronics for a self-reconfigurable soft robot: a flexible mainboard that drives pneumatic actuators, reads a bend sensor, and shares power and I2C communication with neighboring modules across a 96×28mm flex footprint. Took the board through two full revisions - from a hand-bodged first article to an SMD, FPC-connectorized design - hand-assembled every board, and helped integrate them into the physical multi-module platform alongside graduate researcher Joshua Knospler. The work is cited in a forthcoming 2025 publication I co-authored and is part of research currently being evaluated for patenting.

Problem

Requirements & Constraints

Self-reconfigurable soft robots present a unique electronics challenge: the PCB has to fit inside a soft, jointed robotic module, drive actuators and read sensors reliably, and share power and communication with neighboring modules - all while surviving the module's own ~1° of flection during normal operation.

The board also had to be manufacturable on a research budget: two-layer flex, low-profile SMD parts, and a design the lab could hand-assemble itself rather than outsourcing.

Key Requirements

  • Modular PCB footprint must fit inside each soft robotics module (96 × 28mm)
  • Flexible PCB capable of maintaining ~1° flexion for extended periods
  • Support actuator control and sensor input
  • Integrate a bending sensor for future positional data collection and testing
  • Provide reliable power distribution and communication between modules
  • Low-profile design for mechanical integration into the module body
  • Manufacturable using flexible PCB processes and assembled in the lab
Design

Component Selection & V1 PCB Design

I used low-profile SMD components throughout to fit the tight mechanical envelope, and chose the DRV8833 dual H-bridge motor driver for its reliability, availability, and current capacity against the actuator load. Schematics and two-layer PCB layouts were built in KiCad 9 - two layers specifically to stay within the lab's research budget rather than justify a more expensive 4-layer flex stackup.

The V1 "MRSR Mainboard" integrates six functional blocks on one flexible substrate:

  • Motor Driver: 2× DRV8833PW dual H-bridges for bidirectional actuator control
  • Multiplexer: CD74HC4067M 16-channel analog mux, reading multiple module I/O lines back through a single ADC pin
  • PAC (Power/Actuator/Comms) connectors: six pads (red/blue) carrying SCL/SDA/VCC/GND plus actuator outputs between modules
  • Bend Sensor: resistive flex sensor in a voltage-divider with a 4.7k pull-down, read on an analog pin
  • Pneumatics: transistor-driven solenoid valve outputs for the module's pneumatic actuators
  • Backup power: coin-cell + supercapacitor header to hold state through brief power interruptions

Iteration

Design Iteration: Rev A → Rev B

Rev A (V1) came back from fabrication with 2-3 incorrect traces, requiring hand-soldered wire jumpers to restore continuity before the boards were usable - a direct lesson in the cost of skipping a second design review before sending a board out.

Rev B (V2) corrected those errors and went further: through-hole 2N2222 transistors driving the pneumatic valves were replaced with SMD AO3400A MOSFETs to cut footprint and simplify reflow assembly, and the microcontroller interface - previously multiple discrete pads on the mainboard itself - was replaced with a compact 11-pin FPC connector (FH35C-11S, 0.35mm pitch) linking to a separate Seeeduino XIAO daughterboard. Splitting the MCU onto its own board meant a bad microcontroller or firmware revision no longer required re-fabricating the whole mainboard - a real modularity and reliability win.

Fabrication

Hand Assembly & Inspection

Every board was hand-assembled using SMT soldering techniques - solder paste applied and reflowed with a hot plate and heat gun, no reflow oven - then inspected under a microscope to confirm solder joint integrity and rule out trace bridging before a board went into a module.

Integration

Module Integration & PAC Connector Assembly

I collaborated closely with graduate researcher Joshua Knospler to make sure the PCB's form factor matched the module's mechanical design, and assisted in overall module assembly - wiring the PAC connectors that link power, I2C, and actuator lines between adjacent modules, and contributing hardware design refinements as the mechanical build progressed.

Testing & Validation

Electrical Validation & Module Testing

Initial electrical validation confirmed continuity and power integrity across the assembled boards. Modules successfully powered their actuators under load with stable performance, including a pneumatic inflation test of an assembled module. Full system-level validation and long-term flection-cycle testing are planned for the upcoming research phase.

Reflection & Lessons

  • The Rev A → Rev B jump reinforced the value of a second design review and trace verification before fabrication - the bodge wires on Rev A were avoidable
  • Moving to SMD components and the FPC connector improved manufacturability and modularity, a pattern I now default to for future board revisions

Next Steps

  • Add dedicated test points for faster debugging
  • Conduct extended flexion-cycle reliability testing
  • Evaluate thermal performance under sustained load
  • Integrate fault monitoring for improved diagnostics

Research Impact

Extending the Lab's Published Research

Several papers have been published by the Rowan Mechanical Engineering department establishing the foundations of self-reconfigurable soft robotics. The PCB systems described above extend that work by enabling more compact, manufacturable, and modular integration of electronics within the robotic modules.

Publication & IP Status This electronics contribution is included in a forthcoming 2025 publication on which I am a co-author. The underlying research is currently being evaluated for potential patenting and commercialization; while I'm not directly involved in the IP process, my PCB design work forms part of the technical foundation supporting that direction.
