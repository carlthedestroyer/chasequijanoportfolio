Overview

A Year Inside a Technical Training Shop

Subaru's Technical Training group builds the hardware, fixtures, and demo equipment that technicians train on nationwide. As the product design intern, I owned projects end-to-end, from a napkin sketch or a vague ask through CAD, electronics, firmware, fabrication, and delivery, across a very wide range of problem types. Some projects were purely mechanical fixtures, some were embedded systems with wireless networking, and some were shop infrastructure meant to make everyone else's work faster.

That breadth is the throughline of the year: I wasn't specializing in one subsystem. I was the person who could take a request in any domain (electrical, mechanical, or software) and turn it into a working, shippable piece of hardware. The sections below walk through the major bodies of work, roughly in the order they built on each other.

Flagship Project

Bug Box Network: From Bench Prototype to 14-Node System

The centerpiece of the internship was the "Bug Box," a remotely controlled node that toggles electrical components inside training vehicles on and off, letting instructors intentionally introduce faults ("bugs") for technicians to diagnose during competitions and hands-on training. It started as a single test bench built to validate the core electronics before committing to a fleet.

Test Bench (Proof of Concept)

  • ESP8266 microcontroller driving a switching MOSFET to interrupt vehicle circuits on command
  • Current and temperature sensing for closed-loop safety monitoring
  • TP4056 LiPo charge management with an 18650 pack, plus selective diode switching between two power sources
  • Interactive pushbutton for on-bench manual triggering during bring-up

Once the bench validated the switching and power architecture, I scaled it into a proper multi-node network: 14 independent, individually addressable boxes that instructors can arm and fire remotely across a training bay.

Production Node (×14)

  • Xiao ESP32-S3 per node for wireless control, replacing the ESP8266 bench design
  • Dual 18650 cells (6000 mAh) per node with passive cooling and a high-voltage-rated switching transistor
  • 3D-printed enclosure designed for four-point fastening so any node can be opened and serviced in the field without special tools

Assembly & Bill of Materials

Each node is a fully hand-built electromechanical assembly, documented in a step-by-step build guide so any teammate could reliably reproduce it. The battery pack, switching stage, and controller are built up from a 23-line BOM and assembled in roughly twenty sequenced steps:

  • Power: two 18650 cells spot-welded into a pack with polarity-coded nickel-strip leads, wrapped in Kapton tape for electrical insulation
  • Switching: a logic-level MOSFET wired gate/drain/source, gated through a 220Ω resistor off a Xiao ESP32-S3 Plus GPIO pin, with a heat sink epoxied and mechanically fastened for thermal margin
  • Controller mounting: the ESP32-S3 board is epoxied flush into a recessed pocket with the battery leads dressed underneath it, and the antenna press-fit afterward
  • Serviceability: four M3 heat-set inserts pressed in with a soldering iron so the top plate can be removed and re-fastened without stripping plastic threads
  • Strain relief & safety: a rubber grommet for the switched leads leaving the enclosure, heat-shrunk brass spade connectors on the battery leads, and a power-on smoke test (checking for the ESP32's status LED) before final assembly

Tools: spot welder, soldering iron, wire strippers, side cutters, calipers.

Live Control Interface

Each node hosts its own lightweight web server, and the most recent firmware turns one node into an access point running an ESP-NOW mesh dashboard: instructors connect to it directly from a phone or laptop to arm and fire any node in range, no app required. The panel below is a running copy of that dashboard's front end, driven with simulated node data since there's no physical mesh connected to this page. The toggles, uptime counters, and buttons are fully live. Try switching a node's MOSFET power, running Discover, or hitting All Off.

Embedded Systems

Autonomous Demo Vehicle for a Golf-Themed Event

For a company-wide golf-themed event, I built an autonomous vehicle around a modified RC K-Truck chassis to serve as a demo piece. An Arduino Uno reads three Time-of-Flight (ToF) sensors (front, left, and right) to sense obstacle distance and also to derive tire rotation and speed, while a DRV8322 motor driver handles propulsion.

Each ToF sensor works by timing a photon's round trip to a target and back, giving real, effective 3D distance data rather than an inferred estimate. The diagram below is the working principle I used to size sensor placement and update rate for the chassis.

The truck bed was fitted with a custom 3D-printed cargo container carrying the Subaru Technical Training logo, turning a bare RC chassis into a branded piece for the event.

Event Fabrication

Golf Event Hardware: Ramps, Caddy, and Signage

Alongside the demo vehicle, the same golf-themed event needed a set of physical props designed and printed from scratch: modular putting ramps, a golf caddy accessory, and reusable event signage. Each was designed as a small manufacturing exercise in its own right: modularity, tolerancing, and multi-material printing all came up in service of props that only needed to survive a single event.

Modular Golf Ramps

3D printed with modularity as the primary design driver: embedded magnets let sections interlock into longer or branching runs, and internal weights keep each section fixed in place during play. A V-shaped ramp profile creates more than one viable route for the ball, adding a small amount of randomness to an otherwise deterministic prop.

Golf Caddy

A pole-mounted accessory combining a pencil holder for scorecards, a card slot for easy reach, and a golf ball hopper with a protruding tab that makes it easy to retrieve balls sitting low in the hopper. Printed multi-color on a Bambu Lab AMS so the Subaru Technical Training logo came out of the printer pre-finished, with no post-print painting.

Reusable Event Signage

A pole-mounted sign with a clamshell design: several layers of laser-cut and 3D-printed components sandwich a removable, custom laser-cut whiteboard insert. Because the whiteboard pops out independently of the frame, the same sign hardware gets reused across different events just by swapping and rewriting the insert.

Shop Infrastructure

Xtool Laser Cutter Overhaul & Organization Systems

Outside of individual builds, I also invested time improving the shop itself. The Xtool laser cutter's stock setup required swapping hardware to switch between conveyor-belt cutting and regular flat-bed cutting, which discouraged people from using the conveyor mode at all. I modified the machine to support both without any equipment changes, and designed a compact ventilation system, simulated in SolidWorks Flow Simulation before I cut anything, that requires no additional setup between jobs.

A companion material-organization system keeps sheet stock sorted by type and grouped for quick access during cutting, cutting down on the time spent hunting for the right material mid-project.

Component Organizational System

For small electronics, I designed a parametric, 3D-printed bin system to centralize the shop's loose components (resistors, sensors, dev boards, connectors) that had previously been scattered across drawers. Two sliding layers of bins expose different sections without needing to pull the whole tray, and each bin has a labeling section printed directly into the part so labels stay put.

Large-Format Printing & Training Aids

A Full-Scale UFO Prop and 3D-Printed Engine Trainers

One build started as a large-scale UFO prop, originally designed to be 3D printed in multiple riveted sections with an RGB strip revolving around the rim for visual effect. Once the sectioned version proved more expensive to print than it needed to be, I redesigned it as a single component at a smaller scale, cutting print cost without losing the shape or the lighting effect. 3D-Printed TH2B Engine Trainers

To support demonstrations at remote training centers, I produced a batch of 3D-printed TH2B engine models as tactile teaching aids. Beyond just printing the parts, this meant dremeling out clearance pockets on nearly every unit to hit the tolerances needed for the assembly and disassembly instructors rely on during a live demo, a reminder that a "3D printed part" is rarely finished the moment it comes off the plate.

Reflection

What a Year of Breadth Taught Me

The unifying skill this internship built wasn't any single tool. It was moving fluidly between electrical, mechanical, and firmware work on the same project, often in the same week. Scaling the Bug Box from one bench prototype to fourteen networked, field-serviceable units taught me how much design work happens after something "works" once: power budgeting across a fleet, enclosure serviceability, and designing for a technician who isn't the person who built it.

The shop-infrastructure work was a different kind of lesson: some of the highest-leverage projects aren't the flashiest ones. A ventilation duct and a labeled parts drawer don't demo well, but they removed friction from every other project the team touched for the rest of the year.
